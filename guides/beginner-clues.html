A beginner geography clue guide

A fixed order for your first weeks on whereisshe.gg, so the pin is a decision and not a hunch.

Start with the region, not the city

A perfect score on whereisshe.gg is anywhere within 30 kilometres, which usually means the right town. You do not get there by naming a capital first. Name a region, then a country, then a city or a stretch of coast. If you only know the country, pin a plausible part of that country. On the scoring curve, a few hundred kilometres inside the right country is still a solid round. A famous city on the wrong continent is not.

Use the same order on every round until it is habit. The clip can keep looping while you do it.

Five checks, in this order

First, the script and the language. Hangul, Japanese kana, Arabic, Cyrillic, Greek, Thai, and the Latin alphabet with its accents each cut the map differently. Han characters need a second look: simplified characters are used in mainland China and Singapore, and traditional characters are used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau. If you cannot read the words, the letters still count.

Second, driving side. Left-side traffic includes the United Kingdom, Japan, Australia, India, and much of southern Africa and Southeast Asia. Right-side traffic includes most of the Americas and continental Europe. Look at moving cars, parked cars, and the way signs face the lane.

Third, the road itself. Centre-line colour, edge lines, bollards, and the style of a guardrail are supporting clues. Yellow centre lines are common in North America and white lines are common in much of Europe, with plenty of exceptions. Use them to test a country you already suspect.

Fourth, buildings and plants together. Roof tiles, shutters, concrete frames, wooden houses, dry grass, rainforest, cactus, and birch forest each suggest a climate band. A palm tree alone is not a country. A palm tree plus Arabic script plus right-side driving is a much smaller set of places.

Fifth, the sun, if the shadows are clear. Near the middle of the day the sun is to the south in the northern hemisphere and to the north in the southern hemisphere. If you cannot tell the time of day, skip this check rather than inventing a compass.

When the clues disagree

Trust the specific clue over the vague one. A town name on a sign beats a guess based on a red roof. A readable license or a transit system you know beats a feeling about the weather. If two strong clues disagree, look again before you pin. The usual cause is a script you misread, or a car you thought was local because the brand is global.

It is fine to be 200 kilometres off while you are learning. That round is still most of the points. Do not leap to a city because the landscape looks like a postcard of that city. Reading a clip goes deeper on each clue, and cities and countryside explains which checks matter on a street or in a field.

After you guess

Read the place name and the distance. Notice which check would have caught it: the script you skipped, the driving side you were unsure about, the plants you treated as decoration. The next round in the same day is often the same trip, so the country you just learned is a head start, not a spoiler to ignore. The person stays unnamed until the end on purpose. Keep your attention on the place.
